# Behavioral science and virality
Researched: 2026-11-04 · Informs: none yet

Question set: (1) do small ineffective tasks placate people; (2) what motivates hard, effective action; (3) how to get people to join and keep using a tool; (4) what makes it spread; (5) how virality distorts the tool; (6) sharing mechanics.

## 1. Do small, ineffective tasks placate?

Sometimes. The deciding variable is whether the small act is observed by others.

- **Kristofferson, White & Peloza (JCR 2014)**: public token supporters were no more likely to give meaningful support later than a control group that only got the larger request; private token supporters were more likely. Mechanism: public endorsement satisfies impression-management, reducing urgency (moral licensing); private endorsement signals value alignment to oneself (consistency). Exception: among people highly connected to the cause, public token support led to *more* meaningful support than private.
- **Lee & Hsieh (CHI 2013)**: signing an online petition made people ~1.92× more likely to donate to a related cause (consistency), but non-signers donated more to an *unrelated* charity (moral balancing). Signing raised likelihood of donating, not the amount.
- **Kim et al. (clicktivism)**: signers less likely to donate than non-signers (licensing); public clicktivism increased donation intent only among high self-monitors.
- **Moussaoui et al. (Front. Psychol. 2022, 3-study meta)**: activating prosocial motivation → more subsequent support; impression-management motivation → null or negative. Foot-in-the-door effects are unreliable (Burger 1999: many studies show no increase or a decrease).

Net: a public badge as the first act suppresses follow-through for most users. A private, value-framed first act, chained within minutes to a *related* harder ask, increases it. Petitions work only as the first link in that chain.

## 2. What motivates hard, effective action?

Ties, identity, anger, and belief the group can win — not ideology, not crowd size per se.

- **McAdam 1986 (Freedom Summer)**: participation in high-risk activism predicted by strength of ties to other participants/activists, prior activism, and biographical availability — not strength of belief. "High-risk activism is a strong-tie phenomenon." Social media is built on weak ties.
- **van Zomeren, Postmes & Spears 2008 (SIMCA; 182 studies)**: identity, affective injustice (anger), and efficacy predict collective action; politicized identity is the strongest and influences the other two.
- **Ayanian & Tausch (high-risk contexts)**: anger and political efficacy predict action directly and via reduced importance of risk.
- **Hager, Hensel, Hermle & Roth (APSR 2022)**: told their own side's turnout was large, left-leaning supporters became *more* willing to protest (social motives); right-leaning became less (free-riding). Opposing side's size had no effect. → Social proof of own-side participation raises hard-action rates for this audience.
- **Relational organizing effect sizes**:
  - Friend-to-friend text: +8.3 pts turnout (Outvote/Columbia, 2018 midterms).
  - Captains responsible for a named list (Turnout Nation; Green & McClellan 2020): +13.2 pts across four states; +17.1 in Ohio with more support structure. Prior friend-to-friend efforts underperformed "due to a lack of follow-through."
  - Relational email: +12–15 pts vs control; campaign email ≈ 0 or slightly negative (Davenport 2006).
  - Social-pressure mail showing neighbors' turnout: an order of magnitude more effective than ordinary mail; equal to door-knocking (Gerber, Green & Larimer 2008).
  - Vote Rev meta: a friend's GOTV contact +2.5–3 pts; polling-place vote tripling +4.5–10 pts in a primary.
- **"Vitality" = arousal**: high-arousal emotions (anger, anxiety, awe) drive sharing and action; low-arousal (sadness) suppresses (Berger & Milkman 2012). Grief framing depresses action; anger with a target raises it.

## 3. How to get people to join and keep using it (Han)

- Field experiments (Han, APSR 2016): creating a relational context makes people more likely to sign, recruit others, and attend meetings. A reflection prompt ("what did your participation mean?") had a powerful effect on follow-up attendance.
- High-engagement chapters (Han 2014, *How Organizations Develop Activists*): (a) do both organizing and mobilizing; (b) run combined social + political gatherings (all high-engagement sites; none of the low); (c) ask activists to help *plan*, not just attend — ownership; (d) use campaign teams with short-term goals rather than standing committees; (e) coach with reflection and emotional support, not just technical instruction.
- Tell of a low-engagement group: leaders talk about "communications strategy" as the advocacy. A tool that is mostly a comms channel produces a low-engagement movement.
- **Tufekci, *Twitter and Tear Gas***: networked movements scale fast but lack "network internalities" (decision-making capacity, trust); they hit "tactical freeze" — unable to change course after the initial surge; their ease of formation fails to signal capacity that threatens power. Build decision structure before the spike.

## 4. What makes it spread

- **Brady et al. (PNAS 2017)**: each moral-emotional word raised diffusion ~20%; contagion is bounded by group — spreads within ideological networks, not across. → This is a mobilization tool for the aligned 74%, not a persuasion tool.
- **Berger & Milkman 2012**: high-arousal content (awe, anger, anxiety) is more viral; practical value, surprise, interest also help; sadness hurts.
- **Viral challenge structure** (Ice Bucket, 17M videos): nominate 3 named people + 24-hour deadline + documented act + hashtag. This is relational organizing dressed as a game.
- **Lula's Army**: one-way channel, daily dated missions, commanding voice, emotional cause; 50k → 10.8M in three weeks.
- **Kenya**: utility bundled with target — explainer + MP numbers in one place.

## 5. How virality shapes the tool (three hostile forces, one helpful)

1. Virality selects for low-cost public acts — the category that licenses non-action. Unchecked, the tool becomes a badge generator.
2. Scale outruns capacity → tactical freeze. Decision structure must predate the spike.
3. Attention is contested: adversaries don't block messages; they inundate, distract, delegitimize, and sow doubt to produce resignation (Tufekci). Expect infiltration (India farmers' groups).
4. Platform limits: WhatsApp forward ≤5 chats; already-forwarded → 1 chat; "forwarded many times" after 5 hops; 2020 limit cut highly-forwarded messages 70%. Spread runs via groups (≤512), broadcast lists, Communities, channels, links.

Helpful: left-leaning supporters act more when shown their own side's turnout is large (Hager et al.). Real participation counts, by district, raise hard-action rates.

## 6. Sharing mechanics supported by evidence

- Shareable unit = receipt of a completed hard action (meeting held, captain list filled), not an invitation to a soft one. Public display *after* private commitment helps those already identified.
- Nomination beats broadcast: three named people, a deadline, a captain who owns the list. (71 constituents listed 204 people in one HKS pilot.)
- Deep links with pre-filled text into the user's own groups beat forwards under WhatsApp rules; a channel (one voice, dated missions) reached 10M without forward chains.
- A reflection prompt after the action is the cheapest retention mechanic with experimental support.
- Anger with a named target and date travels; grief and generic outrage don't convert.

## Implied funnel (finding, not design)

Private commitment → immediate related hard ask → named-captain recruitment of three → public receipt. Not public-first, broad-first.
